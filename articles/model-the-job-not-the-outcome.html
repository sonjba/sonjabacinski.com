Data & Graph Engineering

Model the Job, Not the Outcome

I wanted my graph to answer the questions the person closing the month actually asks. So I wrote those questions down and followed each one through the data.

Sonja Bacinski · 9 October 2026

I'm building Liquet in public. Once a month it reconciles a firm's cash records against its bank statement. Rules match what they can, an AI agent investigates what's left, and a person approves. The data is synthetic, for a made-up wealth manager.

My first article argued that a model should follow the questions you intend to ask. This one is about how I found those questions, and how they turned into a graph. It's a design, not a result. Nothing here is built yet.

The short version. Following each question through the data showed me what the graph needs, found three gaps before I wrote any graph code, and found two links that no question uses. It also gave me a rule for the agent later: a pattern that repeats can become a rule only if it's how things normally work. A mistake that repeats should be fixed where it starts, not automated.

1. The outcome is not the question

The question I started with was: do the firm's closing balance and the bank's agree? I thought that was the question until I realised a sum answers it. No graph needed.

That's the outcome. The job is what the person closing the month does to get there, and it's a sequence of questions. Which of our entries is this bank line? Why does this one differ? Whose money is it? Can I sign this off? A model is only as good as the question behind it, so knowing what to ask is the core of the work. I decided to model those.

But where do good questions come from? At first it felt like a circle. You rarely ask a question you think can't be answered, and you can't search for a tool if you don't know what to ask.

It turned out to be a loop, and you can step in from either side. I stepped in from the tool side. I already knew from my thesis that keeping dates on records and links lets you ask about order and time, so "what did the firm know, and when?" felt like a fair question. The risk on that side is asking things only because the tool can answer them. A graph could track a live balance, but that isn't the job of the person closing the month, so I left it out. The job comes in from the other side. The person closing the month asks "whose money is this?" without knowing anything about graphs. When I answered those questions by hand, the answers followed links from one record to the next, sometimes five links long. That's the shape a graph is built for.

The same data could sit in tables, and my first article showed that the connections are there whichever way you store them. What the graph changes is where the logic of the job lives. The links the person follows are stored once, with names, instead of being worked out again in every query. The rules and the arithmetic still live in code, but the graph holds how the records connect, and that is the logic the system is built around.

So knowing the tools widens the questions you can imagine, and the job decides which ones are worth building. Experience is going round that loop many times, until you recognise the shapes faster and start noticing new questions.

2. Following the person closing June

The first thing they need is a starting point: what did the firm say its balance was on 31 May? The firm records it, £250,000.00, but my graph had nowhere to keep it. So the very first question showed a gap. The firm's recorded balance became a node of its own, with its date, so a past month's opening balance can still be found later.

Then they open the bank statement. Line 8 says FASTER PAYMENT NAIR P, £5,000. Which of the firm's entries is it? Two could be: Priya Nair's £5,000 on 18 June and the Hart Family Trust's £5,000 on 19 June. To decide, the person walks from the bank line to a client's name: 

BankEntry ←CONTAINS— BankStatement —FOR_ACCOUNT→ Account
          ←ON_ACCOUNT— CashMovement ←HAS_MOVEMENT— Portfolio
          ←OWNS_PORTFOLIO— Client

In plain words: start at the bank line, find its statement and the account it's for, find the firm's £5,000 entries on that account, then follow each one to its portfolio and the client who owns it. "NAIR P" fits Priya, so her entry is the match. The Hart Family Trust's entry is ruled out, with the reason written down.

That's when it clicked for me. The nouns in the person's question became the nodes, and the steps they took to answer it became the paths. If a step has no link in the graph, the graph can't answer the question.

It also answered the question I had been stuck on before writing any code: is a payment a node, an edge or a property? The answer to "which entry is this line?" is a link between a bank line and a cash entry, and a link needs a node at each end. If a payment were drawn as an edge, the match would be an edge pointing at another edge. So the events became nodes: the firm's cash entries, the bank's lines, and trades.

Trades showed why a little later in the month. The firm bought 300 ALBN shares for £3,717.00 on 29 June, and the cash moves on 1 July. The firm's books have it in June, and the June statement doesn't. One trade with two dates, so the trade and its cash entry are separate nodes, each keeping its own date. The fees showed it too. The bank took one transfer of £1,562.50 for five fee entries the firm recorded, totalling £1,598.50. Five entries matched to one bank line is easy when each entry is a node.

The questions kept coming, and the graph had a path for most of them. Why is the ATIT dividend £225 short? Follow the entry to its security, which is overseas, so 15% tax was taken before it was paid. The path finds the security, and code does the maths. Whose money is the £36.00 fee error? Follow it to the portfolio and then to the client, Amelia Hart. The bank's side knows none of these names. That's the part where I think the graph earns its place.

3. What the walk found

At the end of the month the person signs off. Who approved each explanation? My graph couldn't say. It had a status and a date, but no name. That was the second gap.

Fixing it showed me the method still needs judgement. "Who approved it" could be a node (a person) or just a name on the record. The person closing the month only needs the name on the report, so it's a property. If someone later asks "what else did this person approve?", it would become a node. A difference went through the same test earlier. On its own it's just a value on the match, but once it has an explanation, a status and an approver, it becomes a node of its own, a finding.

The third gap is about time. The Hart Family Trust's £5,000 and the ALBN payment are still open at the end of June. A careful person checks next month that they arrived, and nothing in my graph linked one month to the next. So Liquet should remember them. Each month it will start with the items still open from before and look for them on the new statement first. When one turns up, a new link joins the old finding to the bank line that cleared it, which also proves an explanation June could only propose. When one doesn't, it stays open and gets older until a person acts.

Some of these will repeat, and a repeating pattern can become a rule. But only if it's how things normally work, like a purchase agreed at the end of the month settling in the next one. If the same mistake shows up every month, a rule would hide it. That pattern should be fixed where it starts. Version 1 only has June, so I can't test any of this until I add July.

Then I checked it the other way round, looking for parts of the graph that no question used. I found two links: from a portfolio to its account, and from a trade to its security. They're cheap and true, so I kept them, but I've marked them as unused by version 1 so I don't pretend they're needed.

4. What I haven't tested

None of this is built yet, and I haven't drawn the same data with payments as edges to compare. Nothing here shows that the graph helps the agent either. That's the real test still to come: the same month, one agent with graph tools and one with plain tables. The data is also small and synthetic, with five portfolios, one account, one month and six planted differences.

5. What I'd take from it

The question I started with described the end of the job. Writing down the job, question by question, told me what the graph needs, and it found three gaps before I wrote any graph code.

I expect the agent to join the same loop. If it answers the same question every month, like whether a "CUSTODY FEE" line is a fee the firm forgot to record, that answer should become a rule. The agent can suggest it, and a person approves and tests it like any other rule. Then the agent's time goes to the questions nobody has answered yet.

Each good question leaves something behind in the system: a link, a node or a rule. So the smarter the questions get, the better the system gets at answering them. The job is what stops that from turning into a graph of everything.

If your questions are different, your graph may be too.
